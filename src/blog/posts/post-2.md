# Women Who Refused Permission

There is a lineage of women who did not wait for permission. Their stories deserve a quiet page of their own.

## Mythic Fire: Draupadi & Radha

**Draupadi** is introduced to us as beautiful, articulate, intelligent — but her true power is revealed in a courtroom. Until then, she was a passive character in the flow of the story.

When she is gambled away by her husbands in the Mahabharata, she does not plead. She interrogates.

> "Whom did you lose first, yourself or me?"

She has the courage and intelligence to question. For a time when women were only vessels, she stood out as a fire that ended all the clans. She transforms from queen into moral judge of a civilization.

In a war where the men are haunted by doubt, it is the women who glow with conviction. Born of fire, Draupadi becomes the fire that ends dynasties. Yet her strength is lonely. Even Krishna, her ally, is not truly hers.

Power, in her story, is isolation as much as it is brilliance.

#### Radha

**Radha** is longing.

Through Radha we understand devotion as hunger — the human soul aching toward something infinite.

> "Krishan is not just an enticement; he is conjuring from the deep, unspoken desires of the heart."

Her love becomes a metaphor large enough to contain the human condition itself. She is not remembered for possession, but for yearning.

Krishna's life is filled with battles and kingdoms, but Radha's universe has only one center: him.

Radha became a symbol for "longing and yearning."

This was something new I learnt as well: apparently, Krishna was sent to teach human beings "pure love and devotion" — a form of Vishnu and Laxmi.

## Desire and Renunciation: Ambapali & Meerabai

**Ambapali** is one of the earliest women in Indian literature to build an identity outside marriage.

An orphan, trained to be a ganika — a courtesan skilled in beauty, seduction, and performance — she becomes the pride of her city without being daughter, wife, or mother. She controls her sexuality in a society determined to control her.

Then she walks away.

Her encounter with the Buddha forces her to confront impermanence. Wealth, beauty, influence — all temporary. Her renunciation is not defeat. It is autonomy in its purest form.

Ambapali proves that power is not possession. Power is the ability to let go.

![](/blog-images/post-2-ambapali.jpg)

**Meerabai**'s devotion is rebellion disguised as worship.

In a time when young women were forced to marry, cut their hair, and lose their jewels when widowed, she refuses.

At a time when women weren't allowed to do much, she chose herself and her lord. She joined the devotees and went to Varanasi from Chittor with the target to assassinate.

She abandoned family obligations. She was a reluctant bride who carried with her the idol of Krishna that she would love all her life.

Loving Krishna becomes her escape route from marital, social, and royal obligations. She abandons palace expectations and walks toward devotion instead.

To her family, she is a disgrace. To history, she becomes a saint.

Her refusal is quiet — but devastating. She chooses spiritual love over social survival.

## Women in Power: Razia & Jahanara

**Razia Sultana** does not inherit power as regent or placeholder. She claims the throne because she believes she is the most capable ruler.

> "Competence in a woman becomes treason."

She is the first and only female Muslim ruler of Delhi. Unlike queens fighting for the rights of their sons, Razia’s claim to being Sultan is her conviction that she is the most capable of her siblings.

The men she elevates betray her. She stands for her people, yet history nearly erases her.

Razia’s tragedy is not failure — it is the discomfort of a world unable to tolerate a woman who ruled without apology.

**Jahanara Begum** chooses loyalty over empire.

Daughter of Shah Jahan and Mumtaz Mahal, she became the first lady of the Mughal court after her mother’s death — wealthy, influential, politically central.

Yet her greatest act of power is not governance. It is the refusal to abandon her imprisoned father.

> "After Akbar’s reign the Mughal empire became a fabulous kingdom worth fighting for. By Shah Jahan’s time, it was worth murdering for."

After a fire incident, Shah Jahan distributed gold of her weight among the poor, gifted her the port of Surat, and gave her massive annual revenues.

Dara Shikoh was Shah Jahan’s chosen successor and close to her older brother. Aurangzeb disliked this favouritism. He charged Dara Shikoh with apostasy and killed him, while his son was poisoned.

When Aurangzeb seized the throne in 1658, Jahanara voluntarily shared Shah Jahan’s captivity. In an age where empires were murdered for inheritance, she chooses intimacy over ambition.

Her authority lies not in territory, but in allegiance.

## War and Resistance: Lakshmibai & Hazrat Mahal

**Rani Lakshmibai** did not wake up wanting revolution. She wanted inheritance justice.

British law denied her adopted son, Damodar Rao, legitimacy. When the king died, the British tried to take the place under Dalhousie’s "Doctrine of Lapse." The law meant a threat to Bundelkhand.

Rani Lakshmibai sought a diplomatic solution with John Lang. After the Jhansi uprising of 1857, the rebels hid in Jhansi and the British came for her.

She ran with her troops when all hope was lost. With eight-year-old Damodar, she joined Tatya Tope and Rao Sahib in Kalpi.

Hugh Rose was sent to kill them. She died in battle and became a symbol of Swaraj.

A mother becomes a general because the empire leaves her no other option.

She fights not for ideology, but for survival — and in doing so becomes the face of resistance. Her death turns her into a legend. But the legend begins with a woman trying to protect her child.

**Begum Hazrat Mahal** refuses surrender.

Born outside privilege, married into royalty, and pushed into revolt, she becomes one of the few leaders of 1857 who never negotiates submission. Exile swallows her name, but not her defiance.

Orphan to courtesan, born of African origin, married to Nawab Wajid Ali Shah — who later divorced her — she still stood against the British.

By July 1857, Meerut, Kanpur, and Delhi were burning. Rani Lakshmibai fought for Jhansi, and Hazrat Mahal stood against the East India Company.

She was offered a pension to leave, but she said no.

The uprising wasn’t enough; she and her rebels were pushed to Nepal with Nana Sahib. Hazrat Mahal lived in exile in Nepal, the only leader who never submitted to the British.

She dies far from her kingdom. History remembers victors. She remains a footnote.

And yet she never bends.

![](/blog-images/post-2-hazrat-mahal.avif)

---

These women are remembered as myths, saints, queens, rebels. But what unites them is simpler — each one chose herself.

In doing so, they disrupted empires, scriptures, marriages, and tyranny. We now have more figures to admire, more freedoms to claim.

And still the question remains:

Why are women who choose themselves called dangerous?
